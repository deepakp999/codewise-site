<div data-cw-section="learn-box"><h2 id="what-youll-learn">What You'll Learn</h2>
<ul>
<li>Why data readiness is the number one determinant of AI success</li>
<li>The four pillars of data readiness: Availability, Quality, Governance, Architecture</li>
<li>How to run a practical data readiness assessment</li>
<li>What "good enough" data looks like (perfection is not the standard)</li>
</ul>
</div><h2 id="the-meridian-story">The Meridian Story</h2>
<p>Meridian's first "Start Here" initiative — expanding demand forecasting to all product lines — seemed straightforward. The supply chain team had proven the model works on three product lines. Scaling it to all fifteen should be simple, right?</p>
<p>Priya (CTO) ran a data readiness check across the remaining product lines. What she found changed the timeline:</p>
<ul>
<li>Four product lines had sales data in a separate regional ERP system that wasn't integrated with the primary data warehouse</li>
<li>Two product lines had inconsistent product categorization — the same item had different names in different systems</li>
<li>Historical data for three product lines only went back 14 months (the model needed at least 24 months for reliable seasonality patterns)</li>
<li>One product line had no digital records for wholesale orders — they were tracked in spreadsheets updated manually</li>
</ul>
<p>The model wasn't the bottleneck. The data was. Priya estimated it would take three months to clean and integrate the data before the forecasting model could even begin training on the new product lines.</p>
<p>This is one of the most common patterns in enterprise AI: organizations invest in AI technology while underinvesting in the data foundation it requires.</p>
<h2 id="the-data-reality">The Data Reality</h2>
<p>The World Economic Forum reports that fewer than one in five organizations have achieved high maturity in any aspect of data readiness (<a href="https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/01/why-data-readiness-is-now-a-strategic-imperative-for-businesses/">WEF 2026</a>).</p>
<p>More than half of business leaders cite data quality and availability as major challenges to accelerating AI adoption. And 72% say they will prioritize data foundations and pipelines as their fastest-growing AI-related investment over the next 12 months (<a href="https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/01/why-data-readiness-is-now-a-strategic-imperative-for-businesses/">WEF 2026</a>).</p>
<p>The BARC Data, BI and Analytics Trend Monitor 2026 confirms this pattern: data quality management has reclaimed the number one position among priorities for analytics and AI practitioners (<a href="https://www.strategy.com/software/blog/why-data-quality-is-key-to-ai-success-in-2026">Strategy.com/BARC 2026</a>).</p>
<p>The message from the research is consistent: AI success depends more on data readiness than on model selection or technology choice.</p>
<h2 id="the-four-pillars-of-data-readiness">The Four Pillars of Data Readiness</h2>
<h3 id="pillar-1-availability">Pillar 1: Availability</h3>
<p><strong>Question:</strong> Can we access the data we need?</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Assessment Area</th>
<th>What to Check</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Does the data exist?</td>
<td>Is it captured digitally, or only in manual records, emails, or documents?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Is it accessible?</td>
<td>Can the data team query it programmatically, or is it locked in proprietary systems?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Is it integrated?</td>
<td>Can data from different systems be combined? Or does it live in disconnected silos?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Is it timely?</td>
<td>Is data available in near-real-time, or only in monthly batch exports?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Is it sufficient?</td>
<td>Is there enough historical data for the intended use case (typically 2+ years for time series)?</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p><strong>Meridian's finding:</strong> Four product lines had data trapped in a regional ERP system with no integration to the central data warehouse. The data existed but wasn't accessible to the forecasting team.</p>
<h3 id="pillar-2-quality">Pillar 2: Quality</h3>
<p><strong>Question:</strong> Can we trust this data?</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Quality Dimension</th>
<th>What It Means</th>
<th>Example at Meridian</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>Accuracy</strong></td>
<td>Data reflects reality</td>
<td>Product prices matched actual invoiced amounts</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Completeness</strong></td>
<td>No critical fields missing</td>
<td>8% of order records were missing customer region</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Consistency</strong></td>
<td>Same data, same format everywhere</td>
<td>Product "Widget-Pro" was also called "WidgetPro" and "WIDGET PRO"</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Timeliness</strong></td>
<td>Data is current enough for the use case</td>
<td>Wholesale data was updated weekly; model needed daily</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Uniqueness</strong></td>
<td>No unwanted duplicates</td>
<td>3% duplicate customer records across two CRM instances</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>A practical standard: data doesn't need to be perfect. It needs to be <strong>good enough for the specific use case.</strong> A demand forecasting model can tolerate 2% missing values. A fraud detection model may not tolerate any. Define quality thresholds per use case, not as an abstract standard.</p>
<h3 id="pillar-3-governance">Pillar 3: Governance</h3>
<p><strong>Question:</strong> Is there clear ownership, accountability, and control?</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Governance Area</th>
<th>What to Check</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>Ownership</strong></td>
<td>Is there a named person or team responsible for each major dataset?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Access control</strong></td>
<td>Are there clear policies on who can access what data?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Lineage</strong></td>
<td>Can you trace where data came from and how it was transformed?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Privacy and compliance</strong></td>
<td>Is personal data handled according to GDPR, CCPA, or industry-specific regulations?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Classification</strong></td>
<td>Is data classified by sensitivity level (public, internal, confidential, restricted)?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Retention</strong></td>
<td>Are there policies on how long data is kept and when it's archived or deleted?</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>Without governance, even high-quality data becomes a liability — especially when AI systems process sensitive information at scale.</p>
<h3 id="pillar-4-architecture">Pillar 4: Architecture</h3>
<p><strong>Question:</strong> Can our systems deliver data to AI workloads reliably?</p>
<table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Architecture Area</th>
<th>What to Check</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td><strong>Storage</strong></td>
<td>Is data stored in systems that support analytical workloads (data warehouse, data lake)?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Pipelines</strong></td>
<td>Are there automated processes that move and transform data from source to destination?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Integration</strong></td>
<td>Can different systems share data through APIs, event streams, or batch processes?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Scalability</strong></td>
<td>Can the infrastructure handle the volume and velocity that AI workloads require?</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td><strong>Monitoring</strong></td>
<td>Are there alerts and dashboards for pipeline failures, data quality drops, and latency issues?</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
<p>Many organizations built their data architecture for reporting and business intelligence — monthly dashboards, quarterly reports. AI often requires different patterns: real-time data access, larger data volumes, and higher quality standards. Evaluating whether existing architecture can support AI workloads is essential before committing to AI projects.</p>

<h2 id="what-this-means-for-your-organization">What This Means for Your Organization</h2>
<ul>
<li>Run this assessment for each AI use case under consideration, not just once for the organization as a whole. Data readiness varies significantly by domain.</li>
<li>Share the results with your leadership team. Data readiness is not a technical detail — it directly affects AI investment timelines, costs, and likelihood of success.</li>
<li>If the assessment reveals significant gaps, the next lesson (Lesson 9) provides a practical playbook for building a data strategy.</li>
</ul>
<div data-cw-section="mistake-card"><h2 id="common-mistakes">Common Mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Treating data readiness as a one-time project</strong> — Data quality degrades over time as systems change, new data sources are added, and processes evolve. Readiness requires continuous attention, not a one-off cleanup.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming IT owns data readiness alone</strong> — Business teams generate and consume the data. Readiness is a shared responsibility between business and technology.</li>
<li><strong>Aiming for perfection before starting</strong> — "Perfect data" doesn't exist. Define "good enough" thresholds for each use case and start there. Improve iteratively.</li>
<li><strong>Investing in AI before understanding data gaps</strong> — The most common pattern in stalled AI projects: a team spends months building a model, only to discover that the data needed to run it in production isn't available, clean, or governed.</li>
</ul>
</div><h2 id="key-takeaways">Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li>Data readiness is the number one determinant of AI success. More than model selection, more than technology choice, more than talent.</li>
<li>The four pillars — Availability, Quality, Governance, Architecture — provide a practical assessment framework.</li>
<li>Fewer than one in five organizations report high data readiness maturity. This is the most common constraint on AI progress.</li>
<li>Data readiness is not a one-time exercise — it's an ongoing organizational capability.</li>
<li>The scorecard in this lesson is a tool you can use immediately with your leadership team.</li>
</ul>
