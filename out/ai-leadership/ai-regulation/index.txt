<div data-cw-section="learn-box"><h2 id="what-youll-learn">What You'll Learn</h2>

</div><h2 id="the-meridian-story">The Meridian Story</h2>
<p>Elena (General Counsel) presented a regulatory briefing. Her opening point: "The EU AI Act applies to us even though we don't build AI models. We USE AI — and the regulation covers users too. We operate in the EU, which means compliance is not optional."</p>
<p>This was a perspective shift for the team. They'd assumed AI regulation was primarily a concern for technology companies that build AI systems, not for companies that use them as business tools.</p>
<h2 id="the-eu-ai-act--risk-based-framework">The EU AI Act — Risk-Based Framework</h2>
<p>The EU AI Act classifies AI systems by risk level, with escalating requirements:</p>
<p><strong>Unacceptable Risk (Banned):</strong> Social scoring by governments, real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces (with narrow exceptions), AI that manipulates behavior to cause harm.</p>
<p><strong>High Risk (Strict Requirements):</strong> AI used in employment decisions (hiring, evaluation), credit scoring, educational assessment, critical infrastructure management, law enforcement. High-risk systems require: conformity assessments, documentation of training data and model behavior, human oversight mechanisms, accuracy and robustness testing, and registration in an EU database.</p>
<p><strong>Limited Risk (Transparency Obligations):</strong> AI systems that interact with people must disclose they are AI. Deepfake content must be labeled. Emotion recognition systems must inform users.</p>
<p><strong>Minimal Risk (No Specific Requirements):</strong> AI in spam filters, video game AI, industrial optimization. Most enterprise AI falls here.</p>
<p><strong>Key insight for leaders:</strong> Classification depends on the USE CASE, not the technology. The same LLM used for internal meeting summaries (minimal risk) and for screening job applicants (high risk) falls into different categories — with different compliance obligations.</p>
<p><strong>Meridian's assessment:</strong> Invoice processing — minimal risk (internal operational efficiency, no individual impact). Resume screening — high risk (employment decisions affecting individuals). Demand forecasting — minimal risk (internal business optimization). The resume screening tool immediately moved to the governance committee for enhanced review.</p>
<h2 id="us-ai-policy-landscape">US AI Policy Landscape</h2>
<p>The US approach is more sector-specific than the EU's comprehensive framework:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Executive Orders</strong> provide federal guidance on AI safety, security, and trustworthiness</li>
<li><strong>Sector regulators</strong> apply existing laws to AI: SEC for AI in financial services, FDA for AI in healthcare, FTC for AI in consumer protection</li>
<li><strong>State-level regulation</strong> is emerging, with varying requirements across jurisdictions</li>
</ul>
<p>For organizations operating in the US, the practical approach is: monitor federal guidance, understand your sector's regulatory expectations, and build governance that anticipates regulatory evolution.</p>
<h2 id="global-regulatory-trends">Global Regulatory Trends</h2>
<p>Regardless of jurisdiction, several trends are consistent:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Risk-based approaches</strong> — regulation scales with the potential impact of AI decisions</li>
<li><strong>Transparency requirements</strong> — organizations must disclose AI usage, especially when it affects individuals</li>
<li><strong>Accountability</strong> — someone must be responsible for AI system behavior</li>
<li><strong>Data governance</strong> — requirements around training data quality, consent, and privacy</li>
<li><strong>Human oversight</strong> — high-stakes AI decisions require human review</li>
</ol>
<p>Organizations that build governance aligned with these trends will be better prepared as regulations mature.</p>

<h2 id="what-this-means-for-your-organization">What This Means for Your Organization</h2>
<ul>
<li>If you operate in the EU or serve EU customers, the EU AI Act compliance timeline is active. Assess your AI portfolio now.</li>
<li>If you operate primarily in the US, sector-specific regulation applies. Understand your industry's regulatory expectations.</li>
<li>Regardless of jurisdiction, building governance practices aligned with global trends positions you well as regulation evolves.</li>
<li>The responsible AI practices from Lesson 14 (fairness testing, transparency, documentation, human oversight) directly support regulatory compliance.</li>
</ul>
<div data-cw-section="mistake-card"><h2 id="common-mistakes">Common Mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Assuming regulation only applies to AI builders</strong> — The EU AI Act has obligations for deployers (users) of AI, not just providers (builders).</li>
<li><strong>Waiting for regulation to be "finalized" before preparing</strong> — Regulatory implementation is phased. Building governance now is preparation, not premature compliance.</li>
<li><strong>Over-complying for low-risk use cases</strong> — Not every AI tool needs a full conformity assessment. Match compliance effort to risk classification.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring sector-specific requirements</strong> — General AI regulation is one layer. Healthcare, financial services, and other sectors have additional requirements.</li>
</ul>
</div><h2 id="key-takeaways">Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li>The EU AI Act classifies AI by risk level, with escalating requirements. Most enterprise AI falls in minimal or limited risk categories. Employment, credit, and education AI is high risk.</li>
<li>Regulation applies to AI USERS, not just AI builders. Organizations that deploy AI have compliance obligations.</li>
<li>Global regulatory trends converge on: risk-based approaches, transparency, accountability, data governance, and human oversight.</li>
<li>The governance and responsible AI practices from Lessons 12–14 directly support regulatory compliance.</li>
</ul>
