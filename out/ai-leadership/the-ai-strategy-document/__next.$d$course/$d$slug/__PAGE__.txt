</div><h2 id="the-meridian-story">The Meridian Story</h2>
<p>Sarah (CEO) asked Priya and David to prepare a document for the board. "Keep it to two pages. The board doesn't want a technology manifesto. They want to understand: what are we doing, why, what does it cost, what are the risks, and how will we know it's working?"</p>
<p>Priya's first draft was twelve pages with architecture diagrams. David's feedback: "I can't present this. The board will check their phones by page three."</p>
<p>They iterated to a structured two-page document that covered seven sections — enough depth to guide decisions, concise enough to hold attention.</p>
<h2 id="the-seven-section-framework">The Seven-Section Framework</h2>
<h3 id="section-1-vision-and-strategic-alignment-34-sentences">Section 1: Vision and Strategic Alignment (3–4 sentences)</h3>
<p>Connect AI to the company's existing strategic priorities. This isn't about AI — it's about business outcomes that AI enables.</p>
<p><strong>Meridian's version:</strong>
"Meridian will leverage AI capabilities to strengthen three strategic priorities: operational efficiency in manufacturing and supply chain, revenue growth through data-driven demand management, and risk reduction through proactive governance of AI usage across the organization. Our approach is phased, grounded in data readiness, and measured by business outcomes."</p>

<h3 id="section-4-governance-summary">Section 4: Governance Summary</h3>
<p>One paragraph on how AI will be governed. Details come in Module 3, but the board needs to know governance is planned.</p>
<p>"All AI initiatives will be reviewed by a cross-functional governance committee (CTO, CFO, General Counsel). An acceptable use policy for AI tools will be published within four weeks. Data governance standards will be established for each priority use case."</p>
<h3 id="section-5-talent-and-organization">Section 5: Talent and Organization</h3>
<p>What roles exist, what's needed, and how you'll develop capability:</p>
<p>"Meridian's existing data engineering team (4 people) will lead implementation of the forecasting expansion. For invoice processing, a vendor partnership provides capability without new headcount. We will invest in AI literacy development for the broader leadership team over the next six months."</p>

<h2 id="presentation-tips">Presentation Tips</h2>
<p>When presenting AI strategy to a board or senior leadership team:</p>
<p><strong>Lead with business outcomes, not technology.</strong> "We will reduce inventory costs by 10–15% using AI-powered demand forecasting" resonates. "We will deploy a time series ML model using XGBoost" does not.</p>
<p><strong>Acknowledge what you don't know.</strong> "We expect 10–15% cost reduction based on results from three product lines. We'll validate this as we expand." This builds credibility.</p>
<p><strong>Present data readiness honestly.</strong> Boards appreciate candor about gaps — it shows the team has done the homework and isn't overpromising.</p>
<p><strong>Include a "do nothing" comparison.</strong> What happens if we don't invest? Not as a scare tactic, but as context: "Without demand forecasting expansion, we expect inventory carrying costs to remain at current levels of $X million."</p>
<p><strong>Keep it to 2 pages plus an appendix.</strong> The main document should stand alone. Technical details, vendor comparisons, and detailed timelines go in the appendix for those who want to dig deeper.</p>
<h2 id="what-this-means-for-your-organization">What This Means for Your Organization</h2>
<ul>
<li>This template works at any scale — from a startup with 50 people to an enterprise with 50,000. The seven sections apply regardless of size.</li>
<li>If you completed the exercises in Lessons 5–10, you already have the raw material for every section. This lesson is about assembling and communicating it.</li>
<li>A strategy document is only as valuable as its influence on decisions. Share it broadly, reference it when evaluating new AI proposals, and update it every 6 months.</li>
</ul>
<div data-cw-section="mistake-card"><h2 id="common-mistakes">Common Mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Writing the strategy document after launching AI initiatives</strong> — Strategy should precede investment, not rationalize it after the fact.</li>
<li><strong>Omitting the data readiness section</strong> — Boards and leadership teams need to understand that data work is a prerequisite, not a delay. Framing data investment as "phase 1 of the AI strategy" gives it the priority it deserves.</li>
<li><strong>Overpromising timelines and outcomes</strong> — Conservative estimates that you exceed build more credibility than ambitious targets that you miss.</li>
<li><strong>Creating a document that lives in a drawer</strong> — A good strategy document is referenced monthly. If no one looks at it after the board meeting, it wasn't specific enough to guide decisions.</li>
</ul>
</div><h2 id="key-takeaways">Key Takeaways</h2>
<ul>
<li>A strong AI strategy document has seven sections: vision, data readiness, use cases, governance, talent, investment, and metrics.</li>
<li>Lead with business outcomes, not technology. The board cares about results, not algorithms.</li>
<li>Data readiness belongs early in the document — as the second section — because it determines the feasibility of everything that follows.</li>
<li>Keep the main document to two pages. Put technical details in an appendix.</li>
<li>Update the strategy every six months. AI capabilities and organizational readiness evolve — the strategy should evolve with them.</li>
</ul>
